# Chapter 7 — Retire or Donate

Not every laptop gets to be a station. Sometimes the fan sounds like a distant airport, the screen hinge breaks, or the RAM is 2 GB of 2011 vintage and the price of parts begins with a comma. This chapter is by way of saying: **that is fine, and thank the machine for its service.** A laptop that did fifteen years of email and one backup-and-wipe cycle is not a failure; it is a veteran.

But there is a right way and a dusty drawer way to leave it. This chapter covers the honest economics first, then the cleanup, then the two good ends: donation and recycling.

## The first question: revive, donate, or retire?

Quick honest math. A revival project costs about $25–40 (a USB stick, maybe an external drive if none exists) and 3–4 quiet hours. It is worth it when the laptop meets the checklist in chapter 8: 64-bit CPU, 4 GB RAM or an easy upgrade to it, a screen that lights and hinges that hold, a battery that holds *some* charge (or stays permanently plugged in without complaint).

It is *not* worth it when:

- The screen is cracked, dead, or dented enough to blot the view.
- The memory is 2 GB and soldered (non-removable) — even Lubuntu will sigh.
- It needs a new charger or battery whose price is more than the laptop is worth used.
- It is missing its power adapter and the replacement costs more than a used laptop.

There is a middle option people forget: **part-out retirement.** The memory stick (if removable), and especially the hard drive, can be given to another machine of the same era. If a friend or relative repairs the laptops of the neighborhood, veteran drives are welcome gifts. Chapter 2's wipe comes first, always.

## Before it leaves the house, whatever the destination

Three steps, the same for donation or recycling:

1. **Chapter 2's wipe, in full.** For donation, Path B (reset-with-built-in-tools, or the Linux live USB `dd` wipe or encrypt-then-erase for SSDs). Every method in this guide, checked twice for being on the right drive.
2. **The account unlinking** from chapter 2: remove the laptop from any Microsoft/Apple/service device lists, and deauthorize whatever counted authorizations. A future owner must not be able to reach the past owner's accounts, and the accounts must not be locked to a device that now lives in another house.
3. **A photograph.** Genuinely — a photo of the laptop's model label (usually on the underside) with the serial number visible is the last page of its story in the household files, and it reads like a gentle goodbye to people who keep such things. Skip the serial number if the photo will be shared publicly; for a personal archive it simply documents ownership transfer.

One more, for certain donation channels: collect the charger. A donated laptop without its charger is half a laptop.

## Where a laptop can go (United States and beyond)

Choose by what the laptop is:

- **Working or nearly working, parts included:** a local **computer-refurbishing charity**, a school tech club, a library that runs a computer program, a shelter, or a cousin. Organizations such as **Computers with Causes**, **World Computer Exchange**, **Computers for Kids**, and Goodwill's Dell Reconnect program accept laptops in the U.S.; in most of the EU, retailers that sell electronics must take old electronics back, full stop. Search the web for "[your city] donate laptop charity" and the map fills in for your exact county.
- **Dead but respectable:** most **retailers and carriers** run free take-back schemes — in the U.S., Best Buy's in-store electronics recycling accepts laptops up to certain quantities per household per day. Local municipal drop sites take them too.
- **Battery swollen or punctured (a puffed-up trackpad or case is the tell):** do not mail it, do not put it in household trash; take it to an electronics-recycling drop-off that handles batteries — most do — or to a battery retailer's returns counter. Swollen lithium batteries are the one genuinely safety-flavored item in this guide and the reason e-waste bins exist.

One protection for every channel: **never hand over a laptop with the drive inside unless the donation program explicitly re-images every machine it receives** — good refurbishers do exactly this and will say so. When in doubt, chapter 2's wipe settles it forever, and the cost is one quiet evening.

## The e-waste version of chapter 1's spirit

After deletion, the last data risk is not the drive. It is the stuff said around it: email accounts left logged in is a chapter-2 problem, but two quieter ones deserve a hand:

- **Cloud drive residue** — if the Google/OneDrive/Dropbox apps synced the household's paperwork for years, those files still live in the cloud account and are accessible right now, from anywhere, to anyone with the laptop-owning account's password. Confirm the password is strong and only yours (the true last step of chapter 1) before the machine has moved or moved on.
- **The archaeology check, last.** Look one final time in the optical drive slot, the SD slot, and every USB port — SD cards are the classic find, and chapter 1's rescue principle applies to them too.

## What retiring actually feels like

There is a real thing people report after a good wipe-and-donation: relief. The drawer is lighter, the family memory is intact, and a stranger's child may be learning to type on the very keyboard that typed the 3,000-word email about your mother's case. Chairs end their careers as stools; laptops end their careers as stations, gloves, or raw parts, and the **wipe** turns out to be the moment it stops being a risk and becomes a gift.

## Chapter 7 in one breath

Honest math first (revive worth $25 and 3 hours if chapter 8's checklist mostly passes) → wipe on Path B, unlink accounts, photograph the label → donate to a refurbishing charity with the charger, or recycle at a retail/municipal take-back → swollen battery = special route → and once in a while, look back at the empty drawer with satisfaction.
